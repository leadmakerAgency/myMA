A hospital stay can wipe out savings faster than most people expect, and the rules around Medicare Part A feel complicated until you see them in action. If you are wondering, "What does Medicare Part A cover," this article walks through coverage specifics, typical costs, and the practical decisions that follow when you or a loved one needs inpatient care, skilled nursing, hospice, or home health services.

What does Medicare Part A cover and who is eligible

Medicare Part A is often called hospital insurance. It covers care that begins in a hospital or in settings linked to inpatient care, plus certain home-based and end-of-life services. Most people qualify for premium-free Part A if they or a spouse paid Medicare taxes for about ten years of work. If you did not meet that work requirement you can still buy Part A, but you will pay a monthly premium and may face a penalty for late enrollment.

Part A pays for:
- Inpatient hospital care for medically necessary services.
- Skilled nursing facility care, when criteria are met.
- Hospice care for terminal illness when the beneficiary chooses palliative rather than curative treatment.
- Home health services that are medically necessary, intermittent, and ordered by a doctor.
- Limited blood coverage related to inpatient care.

How inpatient hospital coverage works

When you go into the hospital as an inpatient, Part A is the primary payer for facility charges. That includes the room, meals, nursing, general medical supplies, and other hospital services. Part A does not cover physician fees for inpatient care; those are generally billed under Part B. It also does not cover long-term custodial care, meaning care primarily for help with daily living rather than active medical treatment.

Medicare organizes inpatient cost-sharing by benefit periods. A benefit period starts the day you go into a hospital or skilled nursing facility and ends after you have been out of an inpatient setting for 60 consecutive days. If you return to the hospital after a break of 60 days or more, a new benefit period begins. Each benefit period comes with its own deductible and coinsurance structure.

Common patient experience: imagine Sarah is admitted for pneumonia and spends five days in the hospital. Part A covers the facility charges during that stay, but Sarah receives a separate bill from her treating physician. If she is still in the hospital when her benefit period deductible applies, she must pay that amount once during the benefit period. If her stay extends beyond the days covered without coinsurance, additional out-of-pocket costs apply.

Skilled nursing facility coverage explained

Part A covers skilled nursing facility care only when it follows a qualifying hospital stay. That qualifying stay generally means at least three consecutive inpatient days, not counting the day you are discharged. If you meet that requirement, Part A will cover skilled nursing care for a limited number of days if you need skilled clinical services such as physical therapy or wound care.

Coverage in the skilled nursing facility is not open-ended. Part A typically pays for up to 100 days per benefit period, but the patient may be responsible for coinsurance after the first 20 days or so, depending on the current policy amounts. Note that custodial or long-term care that focuses on daily living assistance is not covered when that is the sole reason for placement.

Home health benefits and limitations

Home health services under Part A cover skilled nursing care, physical therapy, occupational therapy, and some types of medical social services when the doctor certifies that home-based care is medically necessary and the patient is homebound. Home health does not include 24-hour-a-day care at home, routine homemaker services, or most lifts and home modifications.

If you qualify for home health through Part A, the home health agency bills Medicare and you typically have no Part A coinsurance for the skilled services. Durable medical equipment for home use is usually covered under Part B. If you have Part A and no Part B, the durable medical equipment portion may pose separate costs.

Hospice care under Part A

Hospice is included in Part A and is designed for terminal illness care that focuses on comfort and quality of life. To receive hospice benefits, two doctors must certify that the patient has a life expectancy measured in months, and the beneficiary must choose hospice care instead of treatments aimed at curing the terminal condition. Hospice covers palliative care, counseling, nursing, medications related to the terminal illness, and short-term inpatient respite care for the caregiver.

Hospice does not cover unrelated medical care intended to cure the terminal condition after the patient elects hospice. If a person needs curative treatment, they can stop hospice benefits and seek those services instead. Hospice programs have a specific set of services and a defined election and revocation process that is different from standard inpatient or outpatient benefits.

Limits, lifetime caps, and special rules to watch

Medicare Part A includes some important limits that are easy to overlook. One is the lifetime limit on inpatient psychiatric hospital care. Medicare covers inpatient psychiatric services, but there is a lifetime cap measured in total inpatient days. Another example is lifetime reserve days for prolonged hospitalization, which are an additional set of days you can use once standard covered inpatient days are exhausted. Those reserve days are limited and each one often comes with higher coinsurance.

The 3-day inpatient rule for skilled nursing facility coverage remains crucial. If you are admitted for observation status rather than inpatient status, those days do not count toward the three-day requirement. That can lead to surprise bills if you expect SNF coverage and do not meet the inpatient threshold.

Costs you should expect and how they are structured

A common question is, "What are the costs associated with Medicare Part A?" Costs fall into three categories: premiums, deductibles, and coinsurance. Many people pay no premium for Part A because they paid Medicare taxes during their working years. Those who do not qualify for premium-free coverage must pay a monthly premium. If you delay enrollment without having qualifying coverage, a penalty may apply when you do sign up.

Beyond premiums, Part A requires a substantial deductible at the start of each benefit period in order to trigger the more generous coverage for the first segment of inpatient days. Once that deductible is met, Part A covers most or all of the cost for a set number of days, then shifts to coinsurance for additional days, and finally to lifetime reserve days with higher coinsurance. Skilled nursing facility coverage often starts with a limited number of days with no coinsurance, followed by days where you pay a per-day coinsurance amount.

Because specific dollar amounts change year to year, check the current Medicare information for exact figures. If you want to estimate your potential out-of-pocket exposure, factor in the chance of multiple hospital admissions within a short period. Each admission could restart the benefit period process, leading to multiple deductibles in a relatively brief timeframe.

How Part A interacts with Part B, Medigap, and Medicare Advantage

Part A and Part B often work together. Part A covers facility costs. Part B handles physician services, outpatient care, and some home health equipment. If you have a Medigap policy, it may pick up many of the Part A coinsurance costs and the Part A deductible, depending on the plan. In contrast, if you enroll in a Medicare Advantage plan, those private plans provide your Part A and Part B benefits under the plan’s rules. An advantage plan may change provider networks, prior authorization requirements, and cost-sharing. The underlying Part A benefit still exists, but the path to payment flows through the private plan.

Common scenarios and practical steps

Scenario 1: Short hospital stay with quick discharge
If you are admitted for a short inpatient stay and discharged in less than three days, you may not qualify for SNF coverage later. Confirm your status with the hospital billing office. If your admission was classified as observation, ask about the reason and how to get inpatient status if clinically appropriate.

Scenario 2: Prolonged hospital stay with transfer to skilled nursing
After a three-day inpatient stay, you are transferred to a skilled nursing facility for rehabilitation. Part A will typically cover a portion of the SNF charges for up to 100 days per benefit period, with you paying coinsurance after the initial covered days. Keep track of day counts and benefit period start and end dates to avoid unexpected bills.

Scenario 3: Choosing hospice
If a loved one elects hospice under Part A, discuss explicitly which services are included and which are not. Hospice covers care related to the terminal diagnosis, but not unrelated curative treatments unless the patient revokes hospice and seeks those services instead.

What to do if a claim is denied or you receive unexpected bills

If Medicare denies a Part A claim or you see unexpected bills, start by requesting a detailed explanation of benefits and inpatient versus outpatient status from the hospital. Review the Medicare Summary Notice or your plan’s explanation of benefits. If a coverage decision still seems incorrect, you can file a formal appeal with Medicare. Hospitals also have patient advocates or billing departments that can review status and code issues. Professional case managers and elder law attorneys can help with complex disputes, especially when large sums are at stake.

How to verify coverage and stay ahead of costs

Before a planned procedure, ask the hospital and your doctor whether the admission will be billed as inpatient or outpatient observation. Verify your eligibility for premium-free Part A through Social Security and check whether any supplemental plan will cover Part A cost-sharing. Keep a record of admission and discharge dates to monitor benefit periods. If you anticipate frequent hospitalizations, consider a Medigap plan that covers Part A coinsurance, or assess Medicare Advantage plans carefully for their inpatient coverage rules and hospital networks.

Key takeaways about what Medicare Part A covers

Medicare Part A covers hospital stays, certain skilled nursing facility care, hospice, and medically necessary home health services, but it comes with limits, eligibility rules, and cost-sharing that vary by benefit period and service type. Knowing the difference between inpatient and observation status, and tracking benefit periods, prevents the most common surprises. If you do not qualify for premium-free Part A, you may pay a premium and could face a late enrollment penalty. For those with premiums already covered through past Medicare taxes, Part A still exposes you to deductibles and coinsurance that can be significant after multiple admissions.

Where to find authoritative, up-to-date information

Medicare’s official website and the Social Security Administration provide current figures for premiums, deductibles, and coinsurance amounts. Hospital billing offices, your Medicare plan’s customer service, and state health insurance assistance programs can answer practical questions and help with appeals. When in doubt, ask for the written policy explanation rather than relying on verbal assurances.

A brief final thought

Understanding what Medicare Part A covers reduces stress when medical events happen. Know your inpatient status, track benefit periods, and confirm how any supplemental coverage applies. Small steps like these can keep bills manageable and let you focus on recovery.
