Most people assume their Medicare coverage stays the same year to year. That assumption costs thousands of dollars for many beneficiaries. Medicare Open Enrollment 2027 is the one calendar window each year when you can review and change Part D drug plans and Medicare Advantage coverage, and small adjustments now can protect you from surprise costs in January.

Whether you already have Medicare or you will become eligible in 2027, understanding the dates, the policy shifts likely to affect benefits and pricing, and the practical steps to compare plans will make this open enrollment period less stressful and more productive. Below are the exact deadlines you need, the changes and trends to watch, and concrete tips to help pick the coverage that fits your health needs and budget.

Key Dates for Medicare Open Enrollment 2027
Medicare Open Enrollment 2027 runs from October 15 through December 7. During this period you can switch between Original Medicare plus Part D and Medicare Advantage plans, change Medicare Advantage plans, and enroll in or switch Part D prescription drug plans. Any plan change you make during these dates generally takes effect on January 1, 2028, provided the plan accepts your enrollment and processes it on time.

There are other enrollment windows that matter too. The Medicare Advantage Open Enrollment Period runs from January 1 through March 31 each year. That period allows people already in a Medicare Advantage plan to switch to a different Medicare Advantage plan or to return to Original Medicare with a Part D plan, and you may only use it once in that interval. Special Enrollment Periods exist for qualifying life events, such as moving out of a plan’s service area, losing employer coverage, or qualifying for Medicaid. If you turn 65 or gain Medicare due to disability in 2027, your Initial Enrollment Period is a separate window around your birthday month.

What could change for 2027 and how it affects you
Regulatory and market shifts often change plan premiums, formularies, networks, and out-of-pocket structures from year to year. Here are the most important trends likely to influence Medicare Open Enrollment 2027 decisions.

Prescription drug pricing and formulary updates
Federal drug pricing policies passed in prior years are already reshaping Part D formularies. Expect carriers to adjust which drugs are placed on lower tiers, to move brand drugs to specialty tiers, and to add or remove prior authorization rules. If you take specialty medications or high-cost brand drugs, the biggest risk is a change in coverage or a shift from a low-cost tier to a higher-cost tier. Review your plan’s formulary for 2028 and compare the total annual cost, not just the monthly premium.

Growth of supplemental benefits in Medicare Advantage
Medicare Advantage plans continue to expand supplemental benefits such as dental, vision, hearing, and certain chronic condition supports. In 2027 you may see more plans offering targeted benefits that address social needs, like transportation to medical appointments or meal benefits after hospitalization. These extra benefits can add real value if you intend to use them, but they should not be the only reason to pick a plan. Always weigh network restrictions, drug coverage, and out-of-pocket maximums first.

Network and provider access
Some plans narrow provider networks to control costs. If you rely on a particular doctor or clinic, confirm they remain in-network for 2028. Moving providers into out-of-network status can raise your costs drastically, especially for ongoing specialty care.

Premium volatility and income-related adjustments
Premiums for Part B, Part D, and Medicare Advantage plans can shift due to changes in the broader healthcare market and projected program spending. Also verify your income-related monthly adjustment amount, or IRMAA. If your reported income from two years ago puts you in a higher IRMAA bracket, your Part B and Part D costs may be higher. If your income has dropped, you can seek a reconsideration or provide documentation to Social Security.

What to look for when comparing plans for 2028
Most people focus on the monthly premium and miss the other costs that determine year-long spending. Compare plans using the following practical lens.

Start with your predictable costs
Make a short list of the prescription drugs you use regularly, including dosage and frequency, and list any scheduled procedures or recurring therapies. Add routine providers you see. Use those specifics to estimate your yearly out-of-pocket exposure, considering copay tiers, coinsurance percentages, and deductible amounts.

Calculate total annual cost, not monthly premium
A plan with a low monthly premium can have much higher drug copays or a large deductible. Add the yearly premium to estimated out-of-pocket costs for drugs and medical care to get a realistic figure. For example, if Plan A has a $0 monthly premium but a $3,000 annual drug and procedural cost, while Plan B has a $70 monthly premium but lower drug copays that bring your projected cost to $1,200, Plan B is clearly cheaper for you.

Check the drug formulary and pharmacy network together
A plan may cover a drug, but only at certain pharmacies or with step therapy. Confirm that your preferred pharmacy is in-network and that your drugs are covered at the lower tiers. If you use specialty medications, verify the specialty pharmacy network and whether drugs require prior authorization.

Verify prior authorizations and quantity limits
If your medication historically required prior authorization, check whether that requirement continues and what documentation will be required. If you travel or split time between states, verify whether out-of-area coverage applies to your medication refills.

Consider star ratings and plan stability
CMS star ratings reflect quality measures including member experience and clinical outcomes. A high-rated plan usually provides more reliable service, fewer coverage problems, and better customer support. Review changes in carrier market share in your area, because new entrants or exits can affect plan stability and cost.

A practical example: how to compare two Part D plans
Imagine you take three drugs: Drug X at $200 a month, Drug Y at $50 a month, and Drug Z at $500 every three months. Plan 1 charges a $20 monthly premium, a $500 deductible, and tiered copays that add up to about $1,200 a year after the deductible. Plan 2 charges a $0 monthly premium, no deductible, but places Drug Z on a specialty tier with 30 percent coinsurance.

On paper Plan 2 sounds cheaper because of the $0 premium. But 30 percent coinsurance on Drug Z costs $1,500 per quarter, far exceeding the savings on premiums. When you add Plan 2’s drug costs to any medical expenses, Plan 1 ends up cheaper by a large margin. Run the math with exact drug prices for all plans you consider, and include expected doctor visits and procedures.

A step-by-step timeline and checklist to prepare
Start early so you can make deliberate choices before December 7.

September: Gather documents and list your needs
Collect a list of current medications including dosages, a record of doctors and specialists you see, and your most recent Social Security or tax documents if you suspect IRMAA status may apply. Request 2028 plan materials from your current insurer when they become available.

Early October: Preview plan notices
Plans usually send an Annual Notice of Change and Evidence of Coverage each fall. Read these documents for your current plan to spot any formulary adjustments, network changes, or benefit additions. Create a short list of alternative plans to compare.

October 15 to November: Compare plans in detail
Use Medicare Plan Finder online, call 1-800-MEDICARE, or meet with your State Health Insurance Assistance Program counselor to compare total annual costs using your medication and care list. If you prefer in-person help, set an appointment early to avoid last-minute rush.

Late November to early December: Make your decision
Choose the plan that minimizes your expected total annual cost and preserves access to your providers. If you decide to switch, submit enrollments early to avoid processing delays.

By December 7: Complete enrollment
Confirm the plan accepted your enrollment, and save confirmation documentation. Note the effective date of coverage, typically January 1, 2028.

Common mistakes that cost money and how to avoid them
Assuming nothing changes. Carrier networks, formulary tiers, and copay structures change yearly. Assume change, check early, and verify coverage for the drugs and providers you use.

Focusing only on the monthly premium. Compare total expected costs. Low premiums can hide high deductibles and specialty drug coinsurance.

Missing prior authorization requirements. If a drug moves to a tier that requires prior authorization, your coverage could be delayed. Confirm prior authorization rules and get paperwork lined up before January 1.

Waiting until the last minute. Processing issues, incorrectly completed forms, and heavy call volumes near the deadline increase the chance of a missed enrollment. Finish your selection several days before December 7.

Overlooking supplemental benefits value. Extra dental, vision, hearing, and transportation benefits provide real value if you will use them. Assess whether those benefits matter to your situation, but do not pick a plan solely for perks if it raises your total costs.

Where to get reliable, no-cost help
If the process feels confusing, free assistance exists. Your State Health Insurance Assistance Program offers one-on-one counseling tailored to state rules and plan availability. 1-800-MEDICARE provides national support and official plan data. Medicare.gov’s Plan Finder allows side-by-side comparisons of costs, formularies, and star ratings.

If you use a broker or insurance agent, confirm they are licensed in your state and that their compensation structure does not bias plan recommendations. Ask for clear explanations of why a plan is recommended for your situation and request cost estimates in writing.

What to watch for after you enroll
After enrollment, monitor your mail and your plan’s online portal for welcome materials and ID cards. Keep receipts and Explanation of Benefits statements for the first few months in case you need to contest a claim or appeal a denial. If you notice incorrect billing or denial of a covered medication, contact your plan’s customer service and document the conversation. If the issue is not resolved, you can file an appeal and request a fast appeal if the situation is urgent.

If your health needs change during the year, check whether you qualify for a Special Enrollment Period. Losing Medicaid, moving residence, or entering a nursing facility are examples of events that can trigger a SEP.

Final short checklist before you finish
Make sure your chosen plan covers your regular medications at an acceptable cost, includes your primary providers in-network, and results in a lower total annual cost than your current plan. Verify whether your plan requires prior authorizations and confirm pharmacy access. Submit enrollment before December 7, and keep proof of submission.

A short, clear ending
Medicare Open Enrollment 2027 is a limited opportunity to align coverage with your health needs and budget for the coming year. Prepare early, compare total costs, and confirm network and formulary details. A little focused work now can prevent surprise bills and provide better access to the care you rely on.
