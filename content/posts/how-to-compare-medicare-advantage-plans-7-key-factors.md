Picking a Medicare Advantage plan by glancing only at the monthly premium is a mistake that can cost you hundreds or thousands of dollars over a year. Many people assume cheaper means better, but the truth is total annual cost, access to your doctors, drug coverage, and how the plan manages care matter far more. If you want to make a choice you can live with for a full year, you need a clear method for comparing plans. How to compare Medicare Advantage plans starts with the right questions and a careful read of each plan's details.

How to compare Medicare Advantage plans: 7 key factors

Below are seven factors that determine whether a Medicare Advantage plan will work for your health needs, finances, and lifestyle. Read each section and imagine how it applies to your typical year of health care, not just this month.

1) Total cost, not just the premium

Monthly premium is only one piece of cost. A plan that advertises a $0 premium may have high copays, a steep deductible, or a low threshold before you pay full price for prescriptions. When you compare plans add these items to get an apples-to-apples number: annual premiums, medical deductibles, primary care and specialist copays, inpatient and outpatient hospital cost sharing, prescription drug copays or coinsurance, and the annual out-of-pocket maximum.

Example calculation: Suppose Plan A has a $0 premium, $2,000 out-of-pocket max, $40 specialist copay, and a $250 deductible for drugs. Plan B charges a $50 monthly premium, a $500 out-of-pocket max, $20 specialist copay, and no drug deductible. If you expect three specialist visits and $1,200 in drug costs annually, Plan B’s lower out-of-pocket and drug structure can easily cost less over the year, even with higher monthly premiums.

Also factor in balance billing and out-of-network charges if you value flexibility. If travel or seeing out-of-network specialists matters to you, a lower premium could prove false economy.

2) Provider network and continuity of care

Ask whether your primary care physician, preferred specialists, and local hospital are in the plan’s network. Provider access is the most immediate quality-of-life difference between plans. An HMO plan typically restricts you to in-network providers and requires referrals for specialists. A PPO plan offers more out-of-network flexibility but often at greater cost.

Do not rely solely on online directories. Call your doctor’s office and ask whether they accept the plan, and confirm how they bill for out-of-network visits if that’s a possibility. If you are in active treatment for a chronic condition, changing plans could interrupt care or force you to change specialists. For someone who sees a cardiologist monthly, continuity matters more than small premium savings.

3) Prescription drug coverage and formularies

Many Medicare Advantage plans include Part D drug coverage, but drug formularies change from plan to plan. How to compare Medicare Advantage plans requires a close look at each plan’s formulary (the list of covered drugs), cost tiers, and prior authorization or step therapy rules.

Start by making a list of the medications you take, including dosages and frequency. Use the plan’s online tool or call the plan to verify coverage and estimate annual drug costs. Pay special attention to high-cost, specialty, or biologic medications; some plans place those drugs on the most expensive tiers or require step therapy, meaning you might have to try a lower-cost drug first.

Also check the plan’s preferred pharmacies and mail-order options. A plan might be affordable only if you use their mail-order service or a specific chain pharmacy. If you travel often or use multiple pharmacies, verify coverage and pricing at locations you use.

4) Covered services and extra benefits

Medicare Advantage plans often include additional benefits that Original Medicare does not cover, such as routine dental, vision, hearing, fitness memberships, transportation to medical appointments, and over-the-counter allowances. These extras can deliver real value if they match needs you would otherwise pay for out of pocket.

However, not all extras are equal. Dental benefits may cover only cleanings and exams but not extensive restorative work. Vision coverage may limit frames or brand options. Check annual limits and waiting periods. For example, a plan that provides $1,000 in dental benefits might sound generous, but if it excludes root canals or crowns you would still pay high bills.

Consider which extra benefits matter to you this year. If you need new hearing aids, a plan offering a hearing aid allowance could reduce costs significantly. If you are generally healthy and rarely need routine dental work, that benefit will matter less.

5) Prior authorization, utilization management, and plan restrictions

Some plans require prior authorization for procedures, imaging studies, or certain medications. Others use step therapy, where less expensive medications must be tried before covering costlier alternatives. These rules control costs for the insurer but can delay necessary care or create administrative friction.

Ask how the plan handles common procedures you might need. For instance, if you have a scheduled surgery next year, check whether the plan requires prior approval and how long approvals take. If you rely on a particular biologic medication, verify whether the plan imposes step therapy or quantity limits. Also review the appeals and expedited review process so you know how to respond if coverage is denied.

Plans that advertise ease of care sometimes earn that reputation because they have fewer prior authorization hurdles. For individuals who value timely access and minimal paperwork, that ease can outweigh small cost differences.

6) Plan quality, star ratings, and member experience

Medicare assigns star ratings to Medicare Advantage plans based on performance metrics such as clinical quality, customer service, and member complaints. While star ratings do not tell the whole story, they provide a quick signal about a plan’s overall performance.

Go beyond the star rating and dig into member satisfaction measures, complaint ratios, and how the plan performs in areas that matter to you, like medication adherence or diabetes control. Reviews and local reports can reveal whether members experience long delays in getting prior authorizations, trouble reaching customer service, or billing errors.

Call the plan with a few questions and evaluate how helpful and clear the response is. This small interaction can be revealing. If customer service staff are slow, evasive, or unclear about coverage, expect that experience to repeat when you need help with claims or appeals.

7) Enrollment rules, plan flexibility, and travel coverage

Different plan types impose different rules about when you can enroll, change plans, or return to Original Medicare. Understanding enrollment windows and restrictions is essential. The Annual Election Period runs each fall, but there are special enrollment periods if you move, lose other coverage, or have other qualifying events.

Consider the plan’s geographic scope. If you plan extended travel or spend winter months elsewhere, check how the plan covers out-of-area care. Some plans limit coverage outside the service area or impose high out-of-network charges. If you divide time between two states, a plan with a national network or broad out-of-area emergency coverage may be preferable.

Also consider whether the plan type matches your tolerance for restrictions. HMOs provide cost predictability but limited choice. PPOs offer more flexibility at higher potential cost. Private fee-for-service plans and special needs plans fit specific circumstances. Choose the type that matches how you want to access care, not the cheapest option.

A practical step-by-step comparison process

With the seven factors in mind, use a systematic approach to compare plans side-by-side.

1. Make a one-page health profile. List primary care and specialists, prescriptions with dosages, typical number of visits, upcoming procedures, and any planned travel. This profile makes comparisons concrete.

2. Gather plan documents. Download each plan’s Evidence of Coverage and summary of benefits. These contain the specifics you need for cost and coverage comparisons.

3. Calculate expected annual cost. Use your list to estimate realistic annual spending under each plan, including premiums, expected copays for visits, estimated drug costs, and any likely hospital or specialist care. Include the worst-case scenario up to the plan’s out-of-pocket maximum, especially if you are at risk for hospitalization.

4. Verify provider participation. Call your doctors’ offices to confirm they accept each plan. Ask whether they ever leave plan networks and whether they accept new patients with that plan.

5. Check drug coverage precisely. Use the plan’s online tool or call customer service and provide the exact medication, strength, and frequency. Ask about any special rules or whether a lower-cost alternative will be required first.

6. Test customer service. Call the plan with a technical question about coverage and note how long you wait, whether you get clear answers, and whether the representative offers to follow up in writing. Good service on a simple call often signals good support when problems arise.

7. Consider nonfinancial priorities. If continuity of care, access while traveling, or a hearing aid allowance is a deciding factor for you, weigh those benefits against cost. A plan that costs more but prevents a switch in specialists or provides necessary extras may be the better choice.

Real-world scenario

Margaret, age 72, takes three daily medications, sees an endocrinologist every three months, and needs routine dental cleanings and occasional hearing aid adjustments. She compared two plans using the steps above. Plan X had a $0 premium but limited dental coverage and a $3,000 drug deductible. Plan Y charged $60 per month but included full dental cleanings, low drug copays, and her endocrinologist in-network. After estimating her annual costs, Margaret found Plan Y would save her about $900 this year and avoid the stress of switching specialists. For her, the higher premium paid for better overall value.

Common comparison mistakes to avoid

- Choosing solely on premium. As noted, a low premium can mask high total cost or poor access to care.
- Ignoring the formulary or pharmacy rules. Small changes in drug tiers or preferred pharmacies can mean big differences at the pharmacy counter.
- Overlooking prior authorization rules. A denied authorization can delay treatment and increase stress and cost.
- Relying only on online directories. Provider listings can be out of date. Confirm with the provider directly.
- Forgetting to check the appeals process. Know how long appeals take and what documentation the plan requires.

Questions to ask when you call a plan

When you contact a plan representative, have your health profile ready and ask direct questions: Will my primary care physician and specialists be in-network? Is my medication covered, and what will I pay using my preferred pharmacy? What is the plan’s out-of-pocket maximum? Are there any prior authorization requirements for my expected procedures? How do I file an appeal if coverage is denied, and how quickly are appeals processed? The clarity of the answers should inform your judgment.

Final checklist before you enroll

Before you commit, make a final pass through the essentials: confirm provider participation, verify drug coverage at your preferred pharmacy, calculate expected annual costs, and ensure you understand enrollment deadlines and rules. If any answer is uncertain, request a written confirmation from the plan that you can keep for reference.

Choosing a Medicare Advantage plan is rarely just about price. It is about matching coverage to your medical needs, financial tolerance, and lifestyle. By focusing on total cost, provider access, drug coverage, plan restrictions, extra benefits, quality ratings, and enrollment rules, you can compare plans in a way that reveals real differences. Use the step-by-step process above, simulate a full year of health care under each plan, and pick the option that gives you the best mix of cost predictability and access to care.

A short, final thought: take the time now to compare thoughtfully. Small differences in plan design become large differences in experience when you need care.
